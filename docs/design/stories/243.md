# S1.8 — Adopt the strict PRIVATE egress posture

**Story:** #243 · **Milestone:** M1 — Kernel and foundation
**Unit of work:** one document or code path carrying the permissive posture
**Supersedes:** `CLAIM-15.4` and Decision 4 of `docs/design/stories/15.md`,
`CLAIM-126.3` and `CLAIM-126.4` of `docs/milestones/M5.md`

S1.2 shipped an egress matrix in which `PRIVATE` data reaches a cloud vendor
when a per-session opt-in is present and the payload is de-identified. That cell
is now removed. No `PRIVATE` data reaches a cloud vendor under any condition.

---

## Problem

The design tree has held two irreconcilable positions on this since before either
was implemented. S1.2 recorded the contradiction as Decision 4, implemented the
permissive side because `CLAIM-15.4` seeded it, and filed the conflict as #235
rather than inventing a reconciliation inside an implementation Story.

#235 then could not land. It was filed as a `type:task` with no parent Story, and
`CONTRIBUTING.md` gives a task branch exactly one destination — its Story branch.
It had none. That is the same structural failure that stranded #14's three
follow-ups until #226 gave them a home, reproduced while trying to avoid it.

Both blockers are now discharged. The substantive one was a decision only the
human principal could take, and it has been taken.

## Vision

One posture, stated once, enforced in code, and unable to drift back. A reader
who searches the tree for what `PRIVATE` may do finds a single answer, and a
maintainer who wants to re-enable cloud egress has to delete a test that says in
plain terms why they must not.

### Decision 2 — the consent mechanism is retained, and made provably inert

The ruling is to keep it. That is the more conservative choice in one sense and
the more dangerous in another, and the danger is worth naming.

Under the strict posture `EgressConsent` cannot change any outcome. A parameter
that is threaded through a security guard, branched on, and never able to affect
the verdict is an invitation: a maintainer reading
`if (!isConsentGranted(consent))` will reasonably infer that granting consent
does something, and the shortest path from that inference to a leak is one commit.

Retention therefore comes with an obligation the deletion option would not have
carried. NEVER-243.6 asserts inertness over the whole consent corpus — granted,
withheld, omitted, malformed, and hostile — so that re-enabling the cloud path
fails a test whose name states in plain terms why it must not be re-enabled.
The parameter survives; the behaviour it used to unlock cannot come back quietly.

This also preserves the property Decision 1 of `docs/design/stories/15.md` bought:
the argument default remains withheld, so a caller who omits it is denied. That
now matters less, because `PRIVATE` is denied either way, but it keeps the
signature honest for whatever consent may legitimately gate later.
